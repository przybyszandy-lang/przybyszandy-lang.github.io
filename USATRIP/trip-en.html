The Geographical Progression

Six states, nine state-line crossings:

Arizona → Utah → Arizona → Colorado → New Mexico → Colorado → Utah → Arizona → Nevada → California

Monument Valley straddles the Arizona–Utah line, and the drive from St George to Mesquite clips 29 miles of Arizona through the Virgin River Gorge — both are easy to miss on a map.

Three things to book or buy before you fly

1. The park pass. Non-US residents can no longer buy the standard $80 annual pass. The Non-Resident Annual Pass ($250) is the one you want — it also waives a $100-per-person surcharge that now applies at Grand Canyon, Bryce Canyon and Zion. Buying it digitally on Recreation.gov avoids the postal wait.

2. Grand Canyon lodging. In-park lodges routinely sell out six to twelve months ahead for late April.

3. A Navajo-guided tour at Monument Valley, if you want one — the Valley Drive is rough dirt and a guided vehicle is the comfortable way to see it in a sedan-based trip.

The Journey02

The Shape of the Week

Two Trips in One

This itinerary is deliberately lopsided. The first four days are built around standing still — long afternoons at the rim, a full day with no driving at all, sunset in Monument Valley. The last three days flip the emphasis: the landscape becomes the thing you drive through, at speed, with a viewpoint every twenty minutes.

Days 1 – 4

Drive light. Stay longer.

Four days averaging under three hours behind the wheel. Two nights at the Grand Canyon mean a full day with no travel leg at all. Monument Valley gets an entire afternoon and evening rather than a photo stop. Mesa Verde and Aztec Ruins are treated as destinations in their own right, not as things passed on the way to somewhere else.

Roughly 670 miles · 12 hours driving over four days.

Days 5 – 7

The road trip proper.

The character changes at Aztec. Day 5 climbs the San Juan Mountains on one of the most dramatic paved roads in North America and finishes in the Utah desert. Day 6 strings Bryce, Zion and the Nevada line together in a single arc. Day 7 is the run to the coast, with the stops chosen on the morning rather than in advance.

Roughly 1,130 miles · 20 hours driving over three days.

Day One

Phoenix → Grand Canyon South Rim

Meeting at Sky Harbor · Interstate 17 · Arrival and settling in

The day

You meet at Sky Harbor at 2:00 PM and drive north. Allow forty-five minutes to an hour between meeting and actually leaving the airport perimeter once the car is collected and loaded — which puts you on I-17 at around 3:00 PM and at the rim between 6:30 and 7:00 PM.

The drive is genuinely handsome for an interstate: the saguaro desert gives way to high chaparral, then to the ponderosa pine of the Mogollon Rim as you climb from 1,100 feet at Phoenix to 7,000 feet at Grand Canyon Village. That is a 6,000-foot ascent in three hours, and it will be fifteen to twenty degrees colder when you step out of the car.

This is deliberately not a sightseeing day. If the timing works you may catch the last light at Mather Point — a five-minute walk from the visitor centre car park — but the canyon is not going anywhere and tomorrow is entirely yours. Eat, sleep, acclimatise.

Time zone: Arizona does not observe daylight saving. In late April the whole state runs on the same clock as California — so no watch change today, and none until Monument Valley.

Places to Consider

Sunset Point Rest Area, I-17
A rest stop with a genuinely enormous view over the Bradshaw Mountains, roughly 70 miles north of Phoenix. Worth considering as the natural first leg-stretch, and it is free.
Montezuma Castle National Monument
A five-storey cliff dwelling tucked into a limestone alcove, three minutes off I-17 at Camp Verde. Worth considering because it is a twenty-minute stop that sets up the Ancestral Puebloan thread running through Days 4 and 5. Covered by the annual pass. Closes 5:00 PM — tight but possible.
Sedona & Oak Creek Canyon
Red-rock country on the alternative route north via SR-179 and SR-89A. Worth considering only if you want a longer, slower first day — it adds roughly an hour and the last stretch is winding.
Flagstaff
A pine-scented mountain town at 7,000 feet, and the last proper supermarket, outdoor shop and choice of restaurants before the canyon. Worth considering for a supply stop if you would rather not rely on in-park shops all week.
Tusayan
The small gateway village a mile outside the South Entrance. Worth considering for a late dinner if you arrive after the in-park kitchens have closed.

Practical

Fuel: fill at Flagstaff. There is fuel in Tusayan and at Desert View, but it is the most expensive on the route.

Pass: the South Entrance station is where you can collect or validate the annual pass if you have not bought it digitally.

Layers: late April overnight lows at the rim hover around freezing — roughly 60°F / 15°C by day and 30°F / −1°C at night.

Day Two

Friday 23 April 2027 · No travel leg

Grand Canyon — A Full Day at the Rim

Sunrise 5:46 AM · Sunset 7:08 PM · Thirteen hours of daylight, no schedule

The only day of the week with no map, no drive time and no arrival deadline. What follows are suggestions, not a schedule — the canyon rewards sitting still far more than it rewards a checklist.

Suggested experiences

Hermit Road is shuttle-only from 1 March to 30 November. Private vehicles are not permitted. The free Red Route runs from the Village Route Transfer stop, takes about 80 minutes for the full loop without getting off, and the first bus is around 8:00 AM. Yaki Point and the South Kaibab trailhead are closed to private cars year-round — use the orange Kaibab Rim Route.

If you want to walk

The Rim Trail is the obvious answer — thirteen miles end to end, but you can join and leave it at almost any shuttle stop. The stretch from Powell Point to Monument Creek Vista is paved, quiet and spectacular.

Going below the rim is a different proposition. The Bright Angel Trail to the 1.5-mile resthouse and back is a reasonable half-day (3 miles, about 1,100 feet of climbing on the return). Remember that every step down must be walked back up, at altitude, and that the return takes roughly twice as long as the descent. In late April the inner canyon is already 25–30°F warmer than the rim.

Light

Sunrise at 5:46 AM, sunset at 7:08 PM. For sunrise, Mather and Yavapai Points face east and are a short walk from the lodges. For sunset, Hopi Point and Pima Point on Hermit Road are the traditional choices — shuttles run until roughly an hour after sunset, so you can stay for the last of the colour without stranding yourself.

Places to Consider

Desert View Watchtower
Mary Colter’s 1932 stone tower at the eastern end of the park, with murals by Fred Kabotie inside. Worth considering for panoramic views and a completely different perspective — and it sits directly on tomorrow’s route, so consider saving it for Day 3.
Tusayan Ruin & Museum
A small 800-year-old Puebloan site with a one-room museum, three miles west of Desert View. Worth considering as a quiet ten-minute counterweight to the crowds at the main viewpoints.
Shoshone Point
A one-mile walk along an unmarked forest service road off Desert View Drive to a rim promontory that almost nobody finds. Worth considering if you want the canyon to yourself for half an hour.
Grand Canyon Village Historic District walking tour
El Tovar (1905), the Santa Fe depot, the Rim Trail through the old railhead. Worth considering for the human history — how a canyon became a destination.
Trail of Time
A paved rim walk between Yavapai and the Village where each metre represents a million years of geology. Worth considering as the most painless way to understand what you are looking at.

Worth knowing

The North Rim is closed. It does not open until 15 May, so it is not an option on this trip — anything you read about North Rim viewpoints is for another year.

Weather. Late April at 7,000 feet is unpredictable. Snow is possible. Expect roughly 60°F / 15°C by day, near freezing at dawn, and wind on the rim.

Condors. California condors are regularly seen around Lookout Studio and the Bright Angel trailhead in the morning — look for the numbered wing tags.

Route decision — please read before setting off

The distance and time on the map (170 miles, 3 hours) correspond to the direct route: Desert View → Cameron → Tuba City → Kayenta → Monument Valley.

Marble Canyon — meaning Navajo Bridge, where US-89A crosses the Colorado 470 feet above the river — lies to the north-west, up US-89 past Bitter Springs. Visiting it properly and then continuing to Monument Valley via Page adds roughly 140 miles and 2½ to 3 hours, giving a day of about 320 miles and 6 hours’ driving.

Both are good days — they are just different days. Option A protects the long afternoon in Monument Valley that this itinerary is built around. Option B trades some of that afternoon for Navajo Bridge, Lees Ferry, the Vermilion Cliffs and the option of Horseshoe Bend at Page.

Option A — as mapped Recommended

Desert View → Cameron → Tuba City → Kayenta → Monument Valley. ≈ 180 miles, 3½ hours. Leave at 8:30 AM, an hour at the Watchtower, the Little Colorado River Gorge overlook near Cameron, lunch in Tuba City, and you are at the tribal park gate by about 2:30 PM local time with six hours of daylight left.

Marble Canyon can be seen — from a distance and from above — on a future trip, or substituted with the Little Colorado Gorge, which is genuinely spectacular and directly on the route.

Option B — via Navajo Bridge

Desert View → Cameron → Bitter Springs → Navajo Bridge → back to Bitter Springs → Page → Kayenta → Monument Valley. ≈ 320 miles, 6 hours. Leave at 7:30 AM at the latest.

You would arrive around 5:00–6:00 PM — still in time for sunset over the buttes, but with no time for the Valley Drive or a guided tour today. If you take this option, consider a morning tour on Day 4 before leaving instead.

Along the way

Desert View Watchtower (25 miles east of the Village, inside the park) is the natural first stop and the last view of the Grand Canyon you will get. From the top you can see the Colorado River, the Painted Desert and, on a clear morning, the Navajo Mountain 90 miles north.

Past the East Entrance the road drops into the Painted Desert — banded badlands in grey, mauve and rust. There are Navajo jewellery stalls at the pullouts; buying directly from the maker is both cheaper and more interesting than the gift shops.

North of Kayenta, US-163 delivers the view everybody knows: the Mittens and Merrick Butte rising out of a flat red plain. Forrest Gump Point, the long straight road shot, is 13 miles north of the park turn-off — and is in Utah, if you want to say you crossed the line.

Monument Valley

The tribal park is sovereign Navajo land, run by the Navajo Nation and not part of the National Park System. Your annual pass is worth nothing at the gate. Entry is currently $10 per person per day, cards only; a separate vehicle charge for the scenic loop has also been reported, so budget for both and confirm on arrival.

The 17-mile Valley Drive is the main self-drive experience — and it is the one part of this itinerary where your vehicle genuinely matters. See the advisory below.

A Navajo-guided tour (typically 1½ to 3 hours, roughly $85–$110 per person) solves the vehicle problem and opens roughly half the park that independent visitors may not enter at all — Mystery Valley, the Big Hogan, Ear of the Wind. Operators are based at the visitor centre and at Goulding’s; booking ahead is sensible but same-day is usually possible in April.

Sunset is at 8:02 PM local time. The terrace at the visitor centre looks straight down the valley and is the easiest place to watch it.

Check road conditions — not ideal for a normal sedan

Valley Drive (17-mile loop). The Navajo Nation describes it as “all natural and dirt road… very rough terrain and deep sand dunes” and states plainly that an SUV or truck is preferable. It is not a technical 4×4 route and passenger cars do drive it, but you would be doing so at your own risk, slowly, with a real chance of scraping the underside or bogging in sand — and rental agreements frequently exclude unpaved roads.

Our recommendation: take a guided tour, or drive only as far as the first few viewpoints near the visitor centre and turn back. Ask at the gate about current surface conditions before committing.

Put your watch forward one hour today. The Navajo Nation observes daylight saving time even though the rest of Arizona does not. Monument Valley runs on Mountain Daylight Time — the same clock as Utah and Colorado, and one hour ahead of the Grand Canyon. Park opening hours, tour times and dinner reservations are all in local Navajo time.

Places to Consider

Little Colorado River Gorge Navajo Tribal Park
A 1,200-foot slot gorge with viewpoints directly off SR-64 between Desert View and Cameron. Worth considering as a ten-minute stop that most people drive straight past; small tribal fee.
Cameron Trading Post
A 1916 trading post with a good restaurant, a serious Native American art gallery and a suspension bridge over the Little Colorado. Worth considering as the practical lunch and fuel stop at the junction of SR-64 and US-89.
Navajo Bridge Interpretive Center, Marble Canyon
You can walk across the 1929 bridge, now pedestrian-only, 470 feet above the Colorado. Condors roost under the span. Worth considering if you choose Option B — it is the reason to go.
Goulding’s Trading Post & Museum
The 1920s trading post that brought John Ford to the valley, with a small museum of the film years. Just west of the park in Utah. Worth considering for an hour before sunset.
Wildcat Trail
The only trail in the park you may walk unguided — 3.2 miles on foot around West Mitten Butte. Worth considering in the cool of early evening; it takes about two hours and gives a sense of scale no viewpoint can.

Practical

Fuel: Cameron, Tuba City and Kayenta. The gap between Tuba City and Kayenta is 73 miles with nothing in between.

Alcohol: the sale and possession of alcohol is prohibited across the Navajo Nation, including Monument Valley and Kayenta. Restaurants do not serve it.

Drones are prohibited on all Navajo Nation land, as is rock climbing and leaving marked routes.

Lodging: The View Hotel sits on the canyon rim inside the park; Goulding’s Lodge is just outside in Utah; Kayenta (24 miles south) has the chain hotels. All three book out early for spring weekends.

The drive

An easy, fast run east on US-160 through Kayenta and Teec Nos Pos, with the Carrizo Mountains to the north and the vast emptiness of the Navajo Nation on both sides. The road crosses directly from Arizona into Colorado a few miles north-east of the Four Corners — you will not pass through New Mexico or Utah on this leg, despite how close both come.

Cortez is the last town before the park and the sensible place for fuel and lunch. The Mesa Verde entrance is eight miles east of it on US-160.

Mesa Verde

Nearly 5,000 known archaeological sites and some 600 cliff dwellings, built into the alcoves of a high green mesa between roughly 600 and 1300 CE and abandoned within a single generation at the end. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1978.

The first thing to understand is the geography. From the entrance station it is 21 miles and about 45 minutes of steep, winding, switchbacked road up to the Chapin Mesa sites. That drive is part of the visit — Park Point, at 8,572 feet, gives a view across four states — but budget for it. Allow a minimum of three to four hours in the park; six would not be wasted.

Important — cliff dwelling tours are not running in April

Ranger-led tours are the only way to enter Cliff Palace, Balcony House, Long House or Square Tower House — and the tour season does not begin until early May (4 May in both 2025 and 2026). On 25 April 2027 they will almost certainly not yet have started. Wetherill Mesa, which includes Long House and the self-guided Step House, also stays closed until late May.

This is not a disaster — it simply changes the shape of the visit. Everything below is open and self-guided, there are no tickets to chase, and the park is quiet. Confirm current status on the NPS Mesa Verde conditions page a fortnight before you travel.

Places to Consider

Four Corners Monument
The only place in the United States where four states meet, half a mile off US-160 on the Navajo Nation. Worth considering as a five-minute novelty on the exact route; separate tribal fee, and the annual pass does not apply.
Park Point, Mesa Verde
The highest point in the park at 8,572 feet, with a short walk to a 360-degree panorama over the San Juans, the Ute Mountain and the La Platas. Worth considering on the way up — it is free and takes fifteen minutes.
Canyons of the Ancients National Monument
Ten miles west of Cortez, with far more Ancestral Puebloan sites than Mesa Verde and almost no visitors. Worth considering if the closed tours leave you wanting more; the Canyons of the Ancients Visitor Center and Museum at Dolores is excellent and covered by the pass.
Cortez Cultural Center
A small museum in town with Ancestral Puebloan and Ute exhibits. Worth considering as a rainy-afternoon alternative and a good place to ask about current park conditions.
Mancos
A small ranching town with a handful of galleries and cafés on the road between Mesa Verde and Durango. Worth considering for coffee on the way out towards New Mexico.

Practical

Pass: Mesa Verde charges an entrance fee, covered by your annual pass. It is not one of the parks with the $100 non-resident surcharge.

Elevation: the mesa sits at 7,000–8,500 feet. Late April can bring snow, and delayed openings after storms are posted on the park website each morning.

Fuel: Cortez or Mancos. There is no fuel inside the park.

Timing: to reach Aztec Ruins before it closes you want to be leaving Mesa Verde by about 2:30 PM. See Part Two.

The drive

Short and pleasant: back down the mesa, east through Mancos, then south on US-550 from Durango across the Southern Ute Reservation and into New Mexico. The state line comes about fifteen miles south of Durango. Aztec is a small town on the Animas River; the monument is a mile north of its centre.

Aztec Ruins

Misnamed by nineteenth-century settlers who assumed anything this well built must be Aztec, this is in fact a Chacoan great house — more than 400 masonry rooms raised, remarkably, in about thirty years around 1100 CE, then reoccupied a century later by people connected to Mesa Verde. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site in its own right.

Two things make it exceptional. The first is that you walk through it — along original passageways, under original timber roofing 900 years old, past mortar still holding the fingerprints of the people who pressed it in. Almost nowhere else lets you do that. The second is the Great Kiva: a 40-foot semi-subterranean ceremonial chamber, reconstructed in 1934 and the only one of its kind you can descend into.

The self-guided trail is half a mile and takes about 45 minutes. With the museum and the fifteen-minute film, allow an hour and a quarter.

Timing is the only real constraint today. The monument grounds close at 5:00 PM and the visitor centre and museum at 5:00 PM, having opened at 9:00 AM. To do it justice you want to be through the gate by 3:30–3:45 PM, which means leaving Mesa Verde by about 2:30 PM. If Mesa Verde overruns, Aztec Ruins is close enough to your hotel to visit first thing on Day 5 instead — but Day 5 is long, so it is better done today.

Entry is free. Aztec Ruins is a fee-free unit of the National Park Service; no pass is required, although your annual pass is of course still valid elsewhere.

Where to stay

Aztec itself is small — a handful of independent motels and a very pleasant historic main street. Farmington, fourteen miles west, has the full range of chain hotels and considerably more choice for dinner. Either works; Farmington is marginally better placed for an early start on Day 5, which matters more than usual tomorrow.

Places to Consider

Durango & Silverton Narrow Gauge Railroad depot
A working 1882 steam railroad in the centre of Durango, with a free museum in the roundhouse. Worth considering for half an hour on the way through, even without riding it.
Historic downtown Durango
Main Avenue’s Victorian shopfronts, the Strater Hotel, and the best coffee and lunch on the day’s route. Worth considering as the natural stop between the two parks.
Salmon Ruins, Bloomfield
A second Chacoan great house, twelve miles south of Aztec, with a small museum and a homestead. Worth considering if Aztec Ruins leaves you wanting more — it is quieter still and privately run.
Aztec Museum & Pioneer Village
An oil-country and homesteading collection with reconstructed period buildings in the town itself. Worth considering as a completely different slice of local history.
Navajo Lake State Park
A large reservoir in the sandstone hills 25 miles east of Aztec. Worth considering for a sunset drive if you arrive with the evening to spare.

Looking ahead

Tomorrow is the longest day of the trip by a wide margin — roughly 440 miles and nine hours of driving, over three mountain passes above 10,000 feet. Fill the tank tonight, pack the car tonight, and plan to leave by 7:00 AM. Please read the Day 5 pages this evening rather than in the morning.

Day 5 at a glance — the heaviest day of the week

Parts One and Two together total roughly 440 miles and nine hours of driving, plus an hour and a half on Rim Rock Drive. That is a genuinely full day and it needs a 7:00 AM start. The reward is that it is also the most spectacular driving day of the trip — alpine in the morning, red rock at lunchtime, Utah desert at sunset.

If you would rather not do all of it, the honest place to shorten is Part Two: see the Day 5 Part Two page for a realistic view of what fits.

The drive north — US-550

This is one of the great drives in North America and it deserves to be treated as a destination rather than a transfer. From Durango the road climbs through the San Juan National Forest over Coal Bank Pass (10,640 ft) and Molas Pass (10,910 ft) into Silverton, a former silver camp sitting in a bowl of 13,000-foot peaks with its main street still unpaved.

North of Silverton comes the Million Dollar Highway proper: eleven miles over Red Mountain Pass (11,018 ft) and down a cliff-edge shelf road into Ouray. There are no guardrails on long sections and the drop is considerable. It is fully paved, maintained year-round and entirely manageable in a sedan — but drive it slowly, use the pullouts, and do not attempt it in poor visibility. Allow substantially more time than the mileage suggests.

Past Ouray and Ridgway the mountains fall away into ranching country, then orchard country around Delta and Olathe, and finally the wide Grand Valley of the Colorado River at Grand Junction.

Check road conditions — seasonal, not vehicle-related

US-550 is a paved state highway and is fine for your sedan. However, late-April snow at 11,000 feet is normal, and Red Mountain Pass closes for avalanche control several times each spring, sometimes for hours. Check cotrip.org (Colorado Department of Transportation) before leaving Aztec.

If the pass is closed or the forecast is poor, the alternative is west on US-64 to Shiprock, north on US-491 to Monticello, then US-191 and I-70 — longer in miles, lower, faster, and it puts you within reach of Moab. Ask at your hotel; local advice on this road is reliable.

Colorado National Monument

Twenty thousand acres of sheer-walled red canyons and free-standing monoliths cut into the edge of the Uncompahgre Plateau, immediately above Grand Junction and Fruita. It is far less visited than it deserves to be.

The whole park is essentially one road. Rim Rock Drive runs 23 miles between the Fruita (west) and Grand Junction (east) entrances, climbing through tunnels blasted in the 1930s and then running along the canyon rim with more than a dozen overlooks. Driven straight through it takes 45 minutes to an hour; with stops, allow an hour and a half to two hours.

Coming from the south-east you will enter at the Grand Junction end and exit at Fruita, which puts you directly onto I-70 westbound for Part Two — the correct direction of travel for this itinerary.

Cathedral Valley — not suitable for a normal sedan, and not possible today

This is the one place where the plan as drawn does not survive contact with the ground, and we would rather tell you now than at a river crossing at dusk.

Cathedral Valley is reached only by the 57.6-mile Cathedral Valley Loop, an unpaved road in the remote northern district of Capitol Reef National Park. The National Park Service states that high-clearance vehicles are necessary and four-wheel drive is often recommended, and that the loop takes six to eight hours of slow driving. The southern access on Hartnet Road requires fording the Fremont River — there is no bridge. Water is normally a foot deep over a rocky bottom, but levels rise sharply in spring, and the loop crosses more than thirty washes with no potable water, no cell coverage and very little traffic.

In a two-wheel-drive sedan this is not a marginal call. Even setting the vehicle aside, six to eight hours does not fit into an afternoon that begins at Grand Junction.

Likewise Crack Canyon. The trailhead is four miles down the unpaved Behind the Reef Road, with a rough final spur that regularly turns back AWD SUVs, followed by a 3–7 mile hike with scrambling. It is a fine outing with the right vehicle and a spare half-day; it is not a stop on a transfer day.

What we suggest instead — and it is not a consolation prize

Your route from Hanksville to Torrey runs along Utah State Route 24, which is fully paved and passes directly through Capitol Reef National Park, along the Fremont River beneath the Waterpocket Fold. You will be driving it in the late afternoon, which is the best possible time. There is no entrance fee for UT-24 itself.

Scenic Byway 12

Consistently rated among the most beautiful roads in America, and the drive is at least as much of an event as the destination. It climbs immediately out of Torrey over the flank of Boulder Mountain to around 9,600 feet through aspen and fir, with long views back east over the Waterpocket Fold and Henry Mountains.

Between Boulder and Escalante comes The Hogback — three miles of road running along a knife-edge ridge with the ground falling away on both sides and no guardrail. Bryce Canyon

Not a canyon at all, but a series of natural amphitheatres eroded into the edge of the Paunsaugunt Plateau, filled with the densest concentration of hoodoos on earth. The park sits between 8,000 and 9,100 feet — the highest ground of your trip.

With two to three hours, the amphitheatre is the priority and the four main viewpoints are close together:

ViewpointNotes

Late April at 8,000 feet

Expect roughly 60°F / 15°C by day and around 30°F / −1°C overnight, with snow entirely possible. Trails inside the amphitheatre hold ice and packed snow well into May, and the Wall Street section of the Navajo Loop is routinely closed in early spring for rockfall and ice — when it is, the Two Bridges side usually remains open. Check the trail status board at the visitor centre before committing, and consider microspikes if you intend to descend.

Getting around: the Bryce shuttle typically starts in early April and is optional — unlike Zion, you may drive to every viewpoint. The 18-mile scenic drive south to Rainbow Point is a further 1–1½ hours return and its upper reaches can stay closed after snow; with today’s schedule, the amphitheatre is the better use of your time.

Fees: $35 per vehicle, covered by your annual pass. Bryce is one of the three parks on this trip with the $100 per-person non-resident surcharge, which your Non-Resident Annual Pass also waives.

The drive

West on UT-12 through Red Canyon, where the road passes through two arches cut in vivid orange rock — a two-minute stop that most people miss. Then south on US-89 past Panguitch and Hatch, and west on UT-9 into the park’s East Entrance.

How Zion works — and why it matters this afternoon

Zion Canyon Scenic Drive is closed to private vehicles whenever the shuttle is running, which covers all of March to late November. In 2026 the shuttle ran from 7:00 AM with the last bus leaving the visitor centre at 6:00 PM in the spring schedule; expect something similar in 2027.

In practice: you cannot drive to the Riverside Walk, the Emerald Pools or the Narrows. You must park at the visitor centre or in Springdale, queue, and ride. A return trip to the far end of the canyon takes about 90 minutes of shuttle time before you walk anywhere.

With two and a half to three hours and a drive to Las Vegas ahead, we suggest not fighting this. The plan below gets you Zion’s two greatest set pieces without ever joining a queue.

West on UT-9 through La Verkin and Hurricane to I-15, then south. Past St George the interstate drops into the Virgin River Gorge — twenty-nine miles that clip the north-western corner of Arizona, threading a deep limestone canyon on what was, when it opened in 1973, the most expensive stretch of rural interstate ever built in the United States. Planet Hollywood Resort & Casino sits at the centre of the Strip, at Harmon Avenue, attached to the Miracle Mile Shops and directly across from the Bellagio fountains. After six days of small desert towns the contrast is total, which is rather the point of ending here.

Entry Passes & Fees

The rules changed significantly on 1 January 2026, and not in a way that favours overseas visitors. The good news is that the arithmetic still lands firmly on the side of buying one annual pass for the car.

What changed — and why your original $250 assumption is right for the wrong reason

Non-US residents can no longer buy the standard $80 America the Beautiful annual pass at all. A separate Non-Resident Annual Pass at $250 was introduced instead. Separately, a $100 per-person surcharge now applies to non-residents aged 16 and over at eleven named parks — three of which are on this itinerary: Grand Canyon, Bryce Canyon and Zion.

Holding the Non-Resident Annual Pass waives that surcharge as well as the standard entrance fee. Without it, two of you would pay $100 each at three parks — $600 in surcharges alone, before entrance fees.

Time zones

This trip crosses one of the more confusing time-zone arrangements in the United States. There are only two changes to make, but neither is where you would expect.

WhenChangeWhy
Days 1–3, ArizonaNo change from Pacific timeArizona does not observe daylight saving, so in April it matches California
Day 3, at Monument Valley+1 hourThe Navajo Nation does observe daylight saving, unlike the rest of Arizona
Days 4–6, UT / CO / NMNo changeAll on Mountain Daylight Time
Day 6, at the Nevada line−1 hourPacific Daylight Time from Nevada onwards

Phones normally handle this automatically, but the Navajo Nation boundary is notorious for confusing them. Confirm tour and dinner times verbally, in local time.

Connectivity

Do not rely on a mobile signal. There is little or none across large parts of the Navajo Nation, on US-550 over the passes, along UT-24 between Hanksville and Torrey, on Scenic Byway 12, and inside most of the parks.

Download offline maps for Arizona, Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada and southern California before you leave the Grand Canyon. Download the NPS app and each park’s offline content at the same time.

Altitude

You go from sea level to 7,000 feet within twelve hours of landing, and spend most of the week between 4,000 and 9,000 feet, touching 11,018 feet on Day 5. Mild headaches, breathlessness on short climbs and disturbed sleep in the first two nights are normal. Drink considerably more water than feels necessary, go easy on alcohol at the Grand Canyon, and treat the first walk below the rim with respect.

Preparation checklist

Before you fly — Non-Resident Annual Pass (digital, Recreation.gov) · ESTA or visa · international driving documentation as required by the rental company · Grand Canyon and Torrey accommodation · Monument Valley tour · travel insurance covering hire-car excess.

In the car — two litres of water per person per day · paper road atlas as a backup · phone mounts and two charging cables · sunglasses and high-factor sun cream · a warm layer within reach, not in the boot · snacks for the long empty stretches.

On the road — check the park conditions page each morning · fill at half a tank · carry the pass and photo ID together · tell someone your plan on Day 5 · watch for elk at dawn and dusk around the Grand Canyon and Mesa Verde.

Planner’s Notes & Corrections

Everything in this itinerary was checked against official sources. Where the original brief did not survive that check, it has been corrected rather than quietly reproduced. This page lists every change and every open question.

Corrections made

ItemAs briefedCorrected position
Annual pass ≈ $250 for a non-resident annual pass Correct figure, but the context has changed. The $250 Non-Resident Annual Pass is now the only annual pass available to non-residents, and a new $100 per-person surcharge applies at Grand Canyon, Bryce and Zion. The pass waives it. Confirmed on nps.gov, January 2026.
Monument Valley fee ≈ $8 per person Out of date. Raised to $10 per person from 1 January 2026, per the Navajo Nation Parks & Recreation official fee page. A separate loop-drive vehicle fee of around $15 is widely reported. No National Park pass is accepted.
Cathedral Valley Major destination on Day 5, Part 2 Not achievable in a sedan, and not achievable that afternoon. The 57.6-mile loop is unpaved, requires high clearance with 4WD often recommended, includes an unbridged river ford, and takes 6–8 hours. Replaced with the paved UT-24 corridor through Capitol Reef, which delivers much of the same country at golden hour. Guided 4×4 alternatives noted.
Crack Canyon Possible stop “on the way” Not realistic. Requires 4+ miles of unpaved Behind the Reef Road plus a rough spur, then a 3–7 mile hike with scrambling. Goblin Valley State Park and Little Wild Horse Canyon offered as sedan-accessible substitutes in the same area.
Mesa Verde “Cliff dwellings and archaeological significance” Ranger-led cliff dwelling tours will not be running. The season starts in early May (4 May in both 2025 and 2026) and entering Cliff Palace, Balcony House or Long House is only possible on a tour. Wetherill Mesa is also closed until late May. The itinerary has been rebuilt around the self-guided Mesa Top and Cliff Palace Loop roads and the overlooks, all of which are open.
Day 3 distance 170 miles / 3 hours, including Marble Canyon Those figures describe the direct route only. Marble Canyon at Navajo Bridge lies up US-89 past Bitter Springs; visiting it and continuing via Page adds roughly 140 miles and 2½–3 hours. Both options are set out on the Day 3 page rather than choosing for you.
Day 5 workload Two legs plus Rim Rock Drive plus Cathedral Valley Overloaded. The two legs alone total ≈ 440 miles and ≈ 9 hours’ driving before any sightseeing. A 7:00 AM start is treated as mandatory and the evening plan simplified accordingly.
State sequence AZ → UT → CO → NM → CO → UT → NV → CA Two crossings missing. Utah at Monument Valley is a brief touch, and the route re-enters Arizona for 29 miles through the Virgin River Gorge between St George and Mesquite. Full sequence: AZ → UT → AZ → CO → NM → CO → UT → AZ → NV → CA — six states, nine crossings.
Grand Canyon access Rim Trail, viewpoints, sunset Noted: Hermit Road is shuttle-only from 1 March to 30 November, and Yaki Point is closed to private cars year-round. The North Rim does not open until 15 May and is unavailable on these dates.
Zion access Zion Canyon, Riverside Walk, scenic drives Noted: Zion Canyon Scenic Drive is closed to private vehicles during shuttle season (March to late November). Canyon Overlook and the Zion–Mount Carmel Highway are drivable and have been made the core of a short visit.
